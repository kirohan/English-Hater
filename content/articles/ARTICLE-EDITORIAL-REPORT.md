## Editorial corrections and cautions

1. **A/An is sound-based.** The lesson consistently uses the first spoken sound, not the first written letter.
2. **“The same” and “a similar” differ.** The source’s “the similar” label is not standard for the intended meaning.
3. **Dozen expression.** “A dozen pencils” is the ordinary form; “a dozen of the pencils” is possible for a specified set.
4. **Many a.** It takes a singular countable noun and a singular verb.
5. **The English.** When it means English people, it takes a plural verb: “The English speak English.”
6. **Initialisms.** The source’s list of “semi-vowel” letter names is not a reliable classification. The article is selected from the spoken first sound: a BBA, an MBA, a PhD, an LLB.
7. **Proper names.** The is used with established names such as the United States, the Netherlands, the Padma and the Himalayas; it is not added to every country, city, road or institution.
8. **Institution names.** “Go to school” is a purpose-based expression. The source groups zoo, bazar/market and mosque with articleless institution words, but standard usage commonly says “the zoo”, “the market” and “the mosque”. “In hospital” is especially associated with British English; American English commonly uses “in the hospital”.
9. **Disease names.** The source’s blanket list is not safe as a modern general rule. “Measles” and “gout” are commonly used without the; “the mumps” and “the plague” are established expressions. A fever, a cough and a headache are countable symptom phrases.
10. **Road names.** Ordinary street names normally take no article (Green Road, Oxford Street). “The road to the station” is a common noun phrase and takes the when specific.
11. **Partitive expressions.** “A piece of advice”, “a bar of soap”, “a loaf of bread” and “an item of clothing” are natural. “A piece of business” is not a general countable equivalent; use “a business matter” or another suitable phrase.
12. **Idioms.** “In an open relation” is not a standard fixed expression for the intended modern meaning; “in an open relationship” is the usual phrase.
13. **Generic singular nouns.** “Man is mortal” is a traditional generic form. For modern neutral English, “Human beings are mortal” or “People are mortal” is clearer. A singular countable noun normally needs a determiner unless it is used in a special title, vocative or fixed expression.
14. **The Panipat.** The city name Panipat normally appears without the. Use “Panipat” for the city and “the Battle of Panipat” for the historical event.
15. **Source examples.** Awkward, dated or grammatically incorrect source sentences were not copied. New examples were written to teach the same rule.
16. **“A most wonderful scenery”.** The source MCQ uses an article before the uncountable noun scenery. That is not standard. Use “a most wonderful scene” or “most wonderful scenery”.
17. **“Went a-hunting”.** The source MCQ appears to test an older “a-hunting” construction. It is archaic or literary and should not be taught as a productive modern pattern for everyday English.
18. **Origin mnemonic.** The source’s “one → a/an” and demonstrative-to-the explanation is treated as a memory aid, not a precise historical account of article development.
